## COLD OPEN

On September 29th, 2025, the United States quietly flipped a switch that reached into more than twenty thousand Chinese companies at once.

Not twenty. Not two hundred. Twenty thousand.

Six weeks later, Washington switched it back off.

This is the story of a weapon built almost by accident, used just long enough to prove how powerful it was — and then holstered, as part of a deal that expires in a matter of weeks from right now.

## PART 1 — THE OLD WAY

For years, America's main tool against China's tech ambitions was something called the Entity List. If the Commerce Department decided a company was a national security risk, it went on the list, and US firms needed a special license to sell it anything sensitive — chips, chipmaking tools, advanced software.

By early 2025, that list had grown to more than 1,300 Chinese entries. But it had an obvious hole. A blacklisted company could simply set up a new subsidiary, under a different name, and keep buying. Researchers found that Huawei alone was connected to at least 149 different entities that had been added to the list over time, one at a time, as fast as compliance officers could catch each new shell.

It was a game of whack-a-mole, and China was winning it on volume.

## PART 2 — THE 50% RULE

So in September 2025, the Bureau of Industry and Security stopped playing whack-a-mole. They introduced what's officially called the Affiliates Rule, and what everyone in the trade compliance world just calls "the 50% Rule."

The logic is simple, and brutal. If a company is fifty percent or more owned — directly, indirectly, or in combination with other blacklisted owners — by any entity already on the Entity List, it's automatically covered by the same restrictions. No new paperwork. No individual investigation. No new name added one at a time.

Overnight, this didn't just add a few companies to the list. It added their entire corporate family trees.

An analysis by WireScreen, a firm that tracks Chinese corporate ownership structures, found that more than twenty thousand Chinese entities were newly caught by this single rule change. Roughly a third of all Entity List companies turned out to have at least one subsidiary crossing that fifty percent threshold — sometimes several.

This is what made the rule so different from everything before it. It didn't just punish companies the US government had specifically investigated. It punished companies connected to them, whether or not Washington had ever heard of them before that morning.

## PART 3 — CHINA'S OWN WEAPON

China had a card of its own on the table, and it wasn't subtle either: rare earths and critical minerals, the raw materials that go into everything from fighter jets to electric motors to the chips America was trying to protect.

One material tells the story better than almost any other: tungsten hexafluoride, or WF6 — a gas used to lay down conductive tungsten films inside advanced memory chips. There's no real substitute for it in modern chip manufacturing at scale.

Starting in February 2025, China tightened export controls on tungsten and related materials. The effect on price was immediate and severe. According to Chinese customs export data, WF6 that was trading at roughly forty-four thousand dollars a ton at the start of the squeeze was priced well above one hundred forty thousand dollars a ton within about a year — and some electronic-grade quotes went considerably higher than that. Two major Japanese producers, who together supplied roughly a quarter of the world's WF6, ran out of feedstock entirely and had to suspend operations in mid-2026.

Two governments, two chokepoints. America controlled the technology China needed to build advanced chips. China controlled a material America needed to build them at all.

## PART 4 — THE TRUCE

Both sides had just demonstrated they could hurt each other badly. So, predictably, they made a deal.

On November 1st, 2025, following a meeting between President Trump and President Xi in Busan, South Korea, the White House announced a trade agreement. Nine days later, the Bureau of Industry and Security formally suspended the Affiliates Rule for one year — the twenty-thousand-company weapon, switched off just six weeks after it was switched on. In exchange, China agreed to suspend the global rollout of its own new rare earth export controls for the same period.

Neither side dismantled its weapon. They just agreed not to fire it — for now.

## PART 5 — THE CLOCK IS RUNNING

Here's the part that should matter to anyone watching this today: that suspension isn't permanent. It runs out on November 9th, 2026.

Unless the truce gets extended, the Affiliates Rule snaps back into force automatically — no new legislation needed, no new announcement required. The same twenty-thousand-plus companies that were suddenly restricted in the fall of 2025 would be right back in the same position, and every company doing business with them would need to have figured out, in advance, which of their partners and suppliers quietly crossed that fifty percent ownership line.

Trade compliance officers describe this rule as "dormant, not deleted." It's still on the books. It's just waiting.

## CLOSE

What makes this story different from a normal tariff fight is the mechanism. This isn't a tax that shows up on an invoice. It's a switch that can turn twenty thousand companies from "business as usual" into "restricted" overnight — and both sides now know exactly how much damage that switch can do, because they've each already flipped one.

The question isn't whether either government has this kind of weapon anymore. They both do, permanently. The only question left is whether, in the next few weeks, anyone pulls the trigger again.
